Uncovering the Equal Probability in Random Walks on n-Gons

Random walks are a fascinating aspect of probability theory, illustrating how stochastic processes can exhibit counterintuitive results. In this scenario, we examine a simple random walk on the vertices of a regular n-gon. The question arises: which vertex is likely to be the last visited? Intuitively, one might assume that the vertex directly opposite the starting point V_0 would be favored due to the geometric distance involved. However, mathematical analysis reveals that every non-starting vertex has an equal probability of \frac{1}{n-1} of being the last visited vertex.

This perhaps surprising outcome arises from the inherent symmetry of the n-gon. Each vertex is structurally the same and experiences identical transition probabilities during the random walk. As you move around the polygon, each step left or right maintains a balance, ensuring that the eventual stopping condition—when all vertices have been visited—does not favor any single vertex based on distance alone. This symmetry ensures that each non-starting vertex effectively has no bias toward becoming the last visited point.

Moreover, simulations conducted reinforce this symmetry perspective, as they consistently show an equal distribution of last-visit outcomes across all non-starting vertices. The randomness of the walk paired with the uniform structure of the n-gon creates an environment where geometric distances lose their influence, leading to the observed equal probability. Understanding this principle can be crucial in various applications, from networking design to optimization problems, where the behaviors of stochastic processes play a significant role.

In conclusion, while our intuition may suggest otherwise, the evidence and mathematical reasoning confirm that the last visited vertex in a random walk on an n-gon remains unbiased by geometric considerations. This insight illustrates the broader principle in probability: that inherent symmetries can yield unexpected but enlightening results. By diving deeper into the mathematics behind these phenomena, we can better appreciate the complexity and beauty of stochastic systems.
