Proving No $C^1$ Injection from $R^3$ to $R^2$: Insights & Tips

In the study of multidimensional calculus, demonstrating the absence of a $C^1$ injection from $\mathbb{R}^3$ to $\mathbb{R}^2$ can seem daunting. However, by utilizing foundational tools like the Inverse Function Theorem, we can tackle this challenge effectively. This article focuses on providing you with a clear pathway to understand the argument backing this theorem and its implications.

To begin, recall that a function $f: A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{3} o \mathbb{R}^{2}$ is considered a $C^1$ injection if it is continuously differentiable and injective. The question at hand prompts an examination of the rank of the differential $Df(x)$ across various scenarios. You have already established the groundwork by analyzing cases where the rank of $Df$ is either $0$ or $2$. This analysis is crucial, as we need to focus our energy on the case where the rank is exactly $1$, particularly around a specified point $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$.

An effective approach here is the application of the Inverse Function Theorem, where the key lies in understanding the implications of rank deficiency. Since the rank of $Df$ is $1$, you’re encouraged to explore the neighborhood around $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ closely. One possible direction is to define new coordinate functions that express $y$ and $z$ in terms of $x$ based on the derivatives available. This will lead you to demonstrate that local vertical slices of the function are not injective, hence confirming that a $C^1$ injection cannot hold.

By weaving together these ideas, you’ll not only validate the absence of a $C^1$ injection but also strengthen your grasp of fundamental geometric concepts in calculus. Engaging deeply with these proofs will not only enhance your problem-solving skills but will also enrich your understanding of the overall structure of mathematical relationships across dimensions.
