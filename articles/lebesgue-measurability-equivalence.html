Exploring the Equivalence of Lebesgue Measurability Definitions

In the realm of measure theory, understanding the conditions that govern Lebesgue measurability is critical for various applications across mathematics and its fields. Two notable approaches to define Lebesgue measurability are through the Caratheodory criterion and the concept of Lebesgue outer measure additivity. While both criteria aim to delineate measurable sets, their equivalence invites a closer examination.

The Caratheodory criterion stipulates that a set $E \subseteq \mathbb R^d$ is Lebesgue measurable if, for any measurable set $B$, the outer measure of $B$ can be expressed as the sum of the measures of the intersections and the complements of $E$. This means that the measure is preserved even when considering disjoint subsets, which is crucial for ensuring the integrity of measurable space.

On the other hand, the definition involving Lebesgue outer measure additivity asserts that, if one can demonstrate that the measure is additive over all pairwise disjoint collections of subsets of $E$, then $E$ qualifies as Lebesgue measurable. This focus on measure additivity emphasizes the additive nature of the outer measure itself, potentially simplifying certain proofs and discussions.

While these definitions may appear different, they ultimately yield the same classification of sets as measurable. The intuition behind this equivalence lies in the fundamental properties of outer measure and how they coincide with the criteria set forth by Caratheodory. Therefore, embracing both perspectives reveals the richness of measure theory and emphasizes the importance of rigor in mathematical definitions.
