Why Some Tangents Do Not Indicate Differentiability

In calculus, the concept of differentiability is closely tied to the behavior of a function at specific points. A common example that illustrates the nuances of this relationship is the absolute value function, f(x) = |x|. At x = 0, the graph of this function features a pointed corner, which leads to interesting questions regarding the existence of tangents and the criteria for differentiability.

The Nature of Tangents

By definition, a function is considered differentiable at a point if it has a unique tangent line at that point. This tangent reflects the function's instantaneous rate of change. The absolute value function, despite having two distinct linear segments that meet at the origin, allows for the drawing of tangents from both sides. The critical issue arises from the fact that while tangents can be drawn, they diverge in slopes at the corner. This results in a lack of a single, well-defined tangent line at x = 0, hence confirming that the function is not differentiable at that point.

Sharp Points and Non-differentiability

The presence of a sharp point or cusp, as seen in the case of f(x) = |x|, signifies a fundamental discontinuity in the slope of the function. When you compute the derivative from the left and right of x = 0, you'll observe that the left-hand slope is -1 while the right-hand slope is +1. Since these two slopes do not converge to a single value, it violates the necessary condition for differentiability, underscoring the fact that existence of tangent lines does not necessarily equate to differentiability.

Conclusion

In summary, while the presence of tangent lines can often imply differentiability, exceptions like the absolute value function remind us that the behavior of a function at critical points can produce complex outcomes. Understanding this nuanced relationship is essential when studying calculus and its applications, particularly in real-world scenarios that rely on smooth and continuous changes.
