Can Vectors in 3D Space Reach Every Point? Unraveling the Mystery

Linear algebra often presents us with fascinating questions about the relationships between vectors and the spaces they can span. In particular, the inquiry into whether any combination of vectors in 3D space can reach all points in that space invites us to explore foundational concepts of linear independence and dimensionality.

Understanding Pivot Positions

The query begins with the setup of a coefficient matrix having a pivot in every row. This situation you described highlights a critical condition in linear algebra. When the number of columns (m) corresponds to the number of rows (n), it implies a square matrix where unique solutions can be achieved via linear combinations of these vectors. Yet, your analysis seems to overlook an essential point: while every row having a pivot suggests at least one unique solution exists, it doesn't rule out the possibility of having more columns than rows (m > n), which leads to infinitely many solutions.

Linear Independence in 3D

Moving to your second query about linearly independent vectors that span 3D space, it's crucial to debunk the misconception that two vectors can cover every point in this three-dimensional area. If two vectors are linearly independent, they can only define a plane in 3D space, meaning they can reach any point within that plane. However, they cannot extend out of that plane to reach every point in 3D space, as a third vector would be necessary to cover the entire space effectively.

The Case for Full Coverage

For full coverage of 3D space, one must consider three linearly independent vectors. These vectors can be arranged in such a way that their linear combinations can form any point in the space. This concept ties back to the rank of the matrix and its ability to transform into reduced row echelon form where the number of leading 1's (pivots) corresponds to the dimension spanned. Thus, understanding the distinctions of the matrix sizes and the relationships between vectors is pivotal in grasping the reachability in linear algebra.
