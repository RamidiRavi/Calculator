Identifying Logic Errors in Number Theory Solutions

In the world of number theory, precision is key, especially when determining the order of elements relative to a given prime modulus. An interesting case has arisen concerning the determination of the order of -2 \pmod{19}, which necessitates a closer inspection of the official solution presented in Exercise 6.4.9 from 'Number Theory Step by Step' by Singh.

The author asserts that since 2 is a primitive root modulo 19, 2^9 \equiv -1 \pmod{19} leads directly to the conclusion that 9 must be the order of -2 \pmod{19}. However, this prompts a critical evaluation: while the author shows that 9 is indeed an index for which the congruence holds, they do not sufficiently demonstrate that smaller indices do not yield a result of congruence to 1.

To validate the claim, one could perform numerical calculations for all integers k such that 1 \leq k \leq 8, checking if any of these indices satisfy (-2)^k \equiv 1 \pmod{19}. If none do, this would affirm 9 as the minimal order. Yet, aside from brute force calculations, employing properties of group theory may provide a more elegant resolution. For instance, the order of an element divides the order of the group formed by the multiplicative integers modulo 19.

In conclusion, while the author's solution is grounded in logical steps, a definitive conclusion regarding the order of -2\pmod {19} requires a thorough investigation of potential smaller orders. Engaging in this kind of deeper verification not only strengthens mathematical foundations but emphasizes the importance of rigor and clarity in problem-solving within number theory.
