Clarifying Minimal Exponential Families in Machine Learning

The concept of a minimal exponential family is pivotal in probabilistic machine learning, yet its interpretation can often lead to confusion, as highlighted in discussions surrounding Murphy's definitions. According to Murphy, a minimal exponential family is characterized by the absence of any non-zero linear combination of the statistics that sums to zero, implying that the elements of the sufficient statistic vector must be linearly independent. This definition sets a foundation for understanding how a model parameterizes distributions.

However, a common point of contention arises when examining specific examples, such as the Bernoulli distribution. In this case, Murphy illustrates that choosing \(\mathcal{T}(x) = [\mathbb{I}\{x = 1\}, \mathbb{I}\{x = 0\}]\) does not yield a minimal representation, as it presents linear dependence among the components. The argument presented relies on the summation \(\vec{1}^{ ext{T}}\mathcal{T}(x) = 1\), suggesting that knowledge of the outcomes between 0 and 1 does not provide independent information, contradicting the earlier definition.

This juxtaposition highlights an essential aspect of understanding linear dependence: the distinction between statistical independence and linear dependence of random variable indicators. While the indicator functions appear to convey distinct information, their summation leads to a logical constraint—a scenario where the total probability must equal one, filling a gap in our understanding of minimal representations.

In resolving these confusions, one must clarify the terms and context. It is worthwhile to engage with other literature and examples that illustrate sufficient statistics more robustly, especially when considering practical implementations in machine learning. Engaging with community queries and scholarly discourse, like those found on platforms such as Cross Validated, can illuminate these nuanced definitions, steering researchers and practitioners toward a more coherent understanding of probabilistic frameworks.
