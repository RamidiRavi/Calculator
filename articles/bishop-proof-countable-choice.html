Does Bishop's Diagonal Proof Reliant on Countable Choice?

In the realm of constructive and intuitionistic mathematics, Errett Bishop’s work, particularly in "Foundations of Constructive Analysis", presents a compelling dive into the uncountability of real numbers. Bishop's proof, found in Chapter 2.2, utilizes a diagonalization argument to show that given any sequence of real numbers, there exists a real number that is not part of that sequence. This raises a significant question for those delving into the philosophical underpinnings of his theorem: does this proof necessitate the use of countable choice?

Bishop introduces two sequences of rational numbers defined by induction at each step, where rational numbers are assigned based on the properties of the previous step’s calculations. However, the crux lies in whether this inductive approach implicitly utilizes countable choice. It can be argued that since the definitions of these sequences depend on the conditions of the sequence \( (a_n)_n \), where one must decide between conditions at each step, the process appears to require a choice that is not strictly mechanical or deterministic.

This observation is particularly intriguing given Bishop's earlier criticism of Brouwer's methods, which he deemed ill-defined when it comes to choice sequences. If Bishop asserts that each sequence must be defined by some law, one struggles to reconcile this stance with the inductive step that seems to call for a choice between valid inequalities. The contention raises an essential dialogue about the foundations and methodologies of constructive analysis and whether it adheres to the strictures it espouses.

In conclusion, Bishop's diagonalization proof serves as a fascinating case study in constructive mathematics. The discussion on whether countable choice is indeed employed provokes reflection on the nature of definitions, choices, and the philosophical stance of mathematics itself. Engaging with these concepts contributes to a broader understanding of not only constructive analysis but also the foundational theories that underlie mathematical reasoning.
