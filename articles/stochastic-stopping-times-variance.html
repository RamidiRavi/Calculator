Unequal Variance in Similar Stopping Times Explained

In stochastic processes, particularly in relation to stopping times, it's common to encounter surprising results that challenge our initial intuitions. A notable case involves two similar stopping times depicting the first occurrence of a tail and the first occurrence of a head from a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials. Despite their shared characteristics, these stopping times can lead to distinct variances in the sums of outcomes associated with them.

Let’s define the process succinctly: consider $X_i \sim Bin(1, rac{1}{2})$, which produces a sequence of 0s and 1s from coin flips. The stopping times $M$ and $N$ mark when the first tail (0) and first head (1) appear, respectively. While it may appear that $S_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N} X_i$ and $S_M$ would yield similar variances owing to the nature of their distributions, the crux lies in understanding the effect of conditional expectations on these sums.

To establish why $Var(S_N) eq Var(S_M)$, you should consider the implications of how the sums are built up through different stopping rules. The first occurrence of a tail (0) is influenced by prior flips ending in heads, thus confining the potential accumulated value of $S_M$. Conversely, the stopping time for heads allows for a broader range of potential outcomes as it tends to occur more quickly, cynically inflating the expected value of $S_N$.

In terms of variance, you must delve into the second moment calculations: beyond $E(S_M) = E(S_N)$, it becomes necessary to evaluate $E(S_M^2)$ versus $E(S_N^2)$. Since $S_M$ aggregates outcomes that are conditional on a delayed event (the first head), its cumulative contribution is inherently less volatile than that of $S_N$, ultimately leading to distinct variances that are not immediately evident.
