Discovering Lehmer's Infinite Fibonacci Identity: A Visual Perspective

In a recent exploration of complex numbers, a fascinating mathematical identity has resurfaced—the infinite Fibonacci arctangent identity, originally discovered by D.H. Lehmer in 1936. The equation not only underlies rich mathematical theory but also intrigues many due to its unique connections to the Fibonacci sequence and complex analysis.

Visualizing the product of complex numbers of the form $z=1+ rac{i}{k}$ has led to the formulation of the product $P=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+ rac{i}{F_{2n+1}} ight).$ This product shows that as the series converges, its argument approaches $ rac{\pi}{4}$. This revelation sparks curiosity about the underlying reasons and implications of this convergence.

As we derive the sum of arctangents, $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \arctan\left( rac{1}{F_{2n+1}} ight)= rac{\pi}{4},$ it becomes evident that there exists a beautifully intricate relationship between Fibonacci numbers and the realm of trigonometry. The traditional algebraic proof elegantly uses identities to create a telescoping sum, ultimately leading to the desired convergence.

However, what’s even more captivating is the existence of a visual proof for this identity, as suggested by contemporary mathematicians. Despite the lack of access to the original paper by Rex H.
