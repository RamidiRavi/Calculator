Proving Lower Bounds: The Infimum Explained

In mathematical analysis, the concept of lower bounds and the infimum (greatest lower bound) plays a crucial role, especially when studying subsets of real numbers. Consider a non-empty subset S that is bounded below. If we define a lower bound l of S subjected to the condition that for every natural number n, there exists an element s_n \in S such that [s_n < l + rac{1}{n}]. Our objective is to prove that l = \inf S.

To begin with, let's take the assumption that l = \inf S. This definition implies that for any element x in S, it holds that x \geq l. Furthermore, let us introduce a small positive number, \epsilon. If we assume that l + \epsilon could also be a lower bound, this would contradict the existence of l being the greatest lower bound, as it would mean there is an element in S greater than l.

By choosing \epsilon = rac{1}{n}, for each n, we can express that there exists an s_n such that s_n < l + rac{1}{n}. As n grows larger, l + rac{1}{n} approaches l, reinforcing the conclusion that no number greater than l can serve as a lower bound, leading to the realization that l encapsulates the least element of the set, fulfilling the definition of infimum.

In conclusion, the argument robustly demonstrates that under the stated assumptions, l must be equal to the infimum of S. This not only solidifies our understanding of lower bounds but also enhances our mathematical acumen regarding the structure of real numbers. While the proof outlined aligns well with traditional approaches, alternative methods such as contradiction or construction could also yield similar outcomes, depending on the context of the problem at hand.
