Unraveling the Mystery of Compact Lie Groups in Topology

Understanding which compact Lie groups $G$ possess only finitely many non-zero homotopy groups is an intriguing question in algebraic topology. To explore this, we must consider the conditions under which the homotopy groups of a Lie group can become trivial.

A compact Lie group is characterized by its smooth manifold structure and the group operations that make it a topological group. The homotopy groups $\pi_n(G)$ reflect the group’s topological properties, especially in terms of its higher-dimensional features. A key criterion is related to the group’s cohomology and whether it has a compact quotient by a certain maximal torus.

It has been established that if a compact Lie group has a compact quotient by a torus, it will typically have finitely many non-zero homotopy groups. For example, groups like $U(n)$ and $SO(n)$ exhibit this behavior. In contrast, non-abelian simple groups tend to have infinitely many non-zero homotopy groups due to their more complex internal structures.

To solidify our understanding, it’s essential to delve deeper into specific examples and classify them based on their algebraic properties. This classification can lead to more significant insights into their structure and the implications for areas like representation theory and physics.
